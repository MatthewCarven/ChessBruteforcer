## The count: 13 games short, found and fixed

  read by `rows`                  89,288,408 games
  Lichess's standard/counts.txt   89,288,421
  short                                   13

Cause: when a frame's end falls inside a game's first line ("[Event" cut, e.g. "[Eve" | "nt \"Rated..."),
neither frame holds the whole line, so the game's start isn't found and the game is glued onto the one
before it. A scan of every frame's first bytes (217 s) found 17 frames beginning part-way through an
[Event line:

  cut after       frames   games lost
  "["                  2            2
  "[E"                 5            5
  "[Eve"               3            3
  "[Even"              2            2
  "[Event"             1            1
  "[Event "            4            0   (the whole "[Event " is in the earlier frame, so it is found there)
  all                 17           13   = the shortfall exactly

Expected: ~2,321 bytes of text a game, so each of the 6,176 frame ends has a 6 in 2,321 chance of falling
in one of the six harmful places: ~16 a month (seen: 13). Over the 46 months queued, ~600 games; over all
165 months, ~1,200.

The 13 glued rows show it: their plies are 155, 230, 178, 153, 203, 218, 115, 205, 122, 116, 171, 118, 50
(the second game's tag lines read as moves); the median game has 62.

Why `check` passed anyway: `get` joined the frames the same way `rows` did, so the two agreed with each
other. Only Lichess's own count could see it.

Fixed in scripts/index-month.py (`rows` and `get` both split the joined text at every game start, so a
game whose [Event line was cut is found where the two frames meet). Tested by scripts/test-index-month.py:
7,892 real games (2013-01) cut into 20 frames on purpose, through every point of an [Event line, mid-move,
a frame with no game start, and a frame whose only game start is cut at its end.

Short duplicates are what you'd expect: games abandoned after a move or two (201,212 games of 1.e4 and
nothing else; 90,441 of 1.d4), and the quick mates every beginner meets (7,258 games of the Scholar's mate
e4 e5 Bc4 Nc6 Qh5 Nf6 Qxf7#). The 20 biggest groups were all confirmed by fetching two games of each.

The long ones (41+ plies, 16,999 games in 3,355 groups; 2013 had none over 20 plies) are bots:

  who played       games
  BOT v BOT       16,448   96.8%   (BOT games are 0.46% of the month)
  BOT v human        525    3.1%
  human v human       26    0.2%   (14 groups of 2-3 games, 42-50 plies)

  by speed: bullet 55.0%, blitz 34.4%, rapid 9.9%, classical 0.7%; 16.0% in tournaments

The longest groups are weak bots (rated ~1200-1600) replaying the same game: the 356-ply pair is one
Ruy Lopez played on 10 July (blitz, 120+3) and again on 31 July (bullet, 90+2), move for move, to the same
mate (black promotes and mates with two queens); a 350-ply group of 39 games runs from 1 July to 31 July
at at least three time controls. Fetched and compared: same moves. Such bots evidently play the same move
in the same position whatever the clock, so a pair of them meeting again replays the whole game. For the thesis:
they are real Lichess games but not independent ones; counts of "how often does this position happen"
may want BOT v BOT games set apart (the flags column already does it).
